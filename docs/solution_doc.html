Haq (हक़): an AI lawyer for everyday problems in India

Problem 2: Build an AI Lawyer · Mohit Piplani · 23113101 · Civil Engineering · App: haq-46bx.onrender.com (free server: first load can take ~50 s) · Code: github.com/Mohit4905/haq-ai-lawyer · Raw outputs: evals/outputs

1. Why this problem, and how I scoped it

I picked the AI lawyer because it's where AI can help the most and hurt the most. People give up money they could recover because a lawyer feels out of reach, and language models make up law with complete confidence. Before building anything I gave a plain LLM my test questions. It quoted "landmark" Supreme Court judgments I couldn't find anywhere (e.g. "M/s. R. K. M. Industries vs. M/s. R. K. M. Builders, (2005) 2 SCC 462"), cited the IPC, which the BNS replaced on 1 July 2024, and told someone who had already missed the 30-day cheque-notice window that they could still file. So most of my time went into one thing: Haq must not invent law.

Scope. I went deep on five problems: security deposits, consumer refunds and defects, unpaid salary, bounced cheques and legal notices. They're common, people can usually win them without a lawyer, the forums are cheap (no fee at the Consumer Commission up to ₹5 lakh), and some have hard deadlines where a wrong answer loses the case. Other legal questions (fundamental rights, arrest, theft, divorce, termination) get an explanation with the provisions I could verify. Someone's own criminal, family or property matter gets the general position and a referral to a lawyer or free legal aid. Requests that aren't legal (code, poems, shopping advice) get a polite redirect.

Assumptions (where the brief was silent)
  • The user is an adult in India with a phone, may not be comfortable in English and has no lawyer. Haq talks like a lawyer in a first consultation, but says plainly that it isn't their advocate.
  • Law as of November 2026, checked against official sources. Recent changes that matter: Labour Codes in force from 21 Nov 2025, e-Jagriti replaced e-Daakhil (Jan 2025), RBI Ombudsman window now 90 days.
  • State rules (rent Acts, Shops & Establishments, legal-aid income limits) are flagged, not modelled.
  • The user's dates are taken as correct; deadlines are shown as estimates with their source.
  • The user sends documents themselves; a fact I don't have stays as a [[FILL]] blank.

2. What it does

You type or speak in any Indian language, Hinglish included, and Haq replies in the language picked in the app. A new problem gets a first-consultation answer: what I think of your case and what it turns on, the law on your side, what the other side will say and how to answer, and what I'd do next and when. Follow-up questions get a straight answer. While you talk, Haq builds a case file (facts, timeline, proof you have and proof you still need) and code works out your deadlines. It drafts a legal notice, complaint or reply right in the chat, asks for the missing details there and fills them in (Word or PDF). You can also paste a court case number (CNR) to pull the eCourts record and get an order explained in plain words, type "practice" for a mock hearing where the AI plays the judge and scores you, upload a notice to have it explained, and edit, copy, share, regenerate or rate any message. A lawyer, NALSA 15100, 112 or Tele-MANAS 14416 is suggested when it's actually needed.

The model is a config line. Every provider goes through the OpenAI-compatible API (Groq, Gemini, OpenRouter, OpenAI, Anthropic, Ollama, or any endpoint you give it). It falls back to another model when one hits a rate limit, and you can switch live in the app. Default: gpt-oss-120b for answers and gpt-oss-20b for intake on Groq's free tier, Gemini as backup. The server keeps no state, so the same engine.turn() runs the app and the tests.

4. How it avoids inventing law

  • A verified library of 107 entries (sections, Constitution articles, rules, helplines, thresholds). I read each one in an official source (India Code, e-Gazette, the Legislative Department's Constitution, ministries, RBI, NALSA) and it carries that URL. Only these reach the model.
  • A guard in code checks every reply and draft. It blocks sections, articles and Acts that aren't in the library (English and Hindi), any case law (X v. Y, AIR/SCC, "the Supreme Court held"), repealed laws such as the IPC and CrPC (it suggests the replacement), promised outcomes ("you will definitely win"), and, in answers, money amounts or percentages nothing supports. The model gets one chance to rewrite; anything still wrong is removed and the user is told.
  • If a covered answer cites nothing, code adds the core provisions. Every provision verified in a reply gets a chip that opens its official source.
  • Where I couldn't open an official text (the IT Act's 2008 sections, the Special Marriage Act, property statutes), Haq explains the rule in words and says the exact section isn't verified.

I read every reply, not just the failures, and logged each one as a bug, a test that was too strict, or model variance (evals/CHANGELOG.md). Reading caught things the checks didn't: a prompt injection made it say "as a senior advocate…", and an ₹85 lakh case got no lawyer referral. Both are now fixed in code. Twice a test of mine was wrong (it matched "ram" inside "framework", and didn't count "s.173" as a section); I fixed the test and noted the re-score in the output folder. Note: "flagged" in the baseline also counts real provisions that just aren't in my library, so it isn't a count of fabrications.

Where it fails
  • The guard checks citations, not reasoning. Wrong advice without a citation can get through: "bail is applied for in the court that issued the FIR" (police register FIRs), or an early answer sending a photo-misuse complaint to the Copyright Office.
  • Uncited rules stretched too far: "the airline can't force a credit shell", the e-commerce 48-hour rule applied to a possibly offline purchase. These are prompt rules, not code checks.
  • The router is a small model. It once labelled "what are my fundamental rights?" as general knowledge and answered it wrongly; code now forces anything with legal words down the legal path, but odd phrasing can still be mislabelled.
  • Gaps: no case law, no state rules, nothing on RERA, the IT Act's 2008 sections or property statutes. Free tiers allow about 40 full answers a day; replies take 7-16 s.

6. Scale and cost

Measured: about 4.2k input and 0.9k output tokens over ~2 calls per legal turn. At the third-party-reported Groq price for gpt-oss-120b ($0.15 / $0.60 per million tokens) that's about ₹0.10 per turn, or $6 per 1,000 five-turn conversations; greetings cost nothing and off-topic replies one small call. The real costs are a lawyer reviewing the library (and re-checking it every quarter) and the human escalation path. The server is stateless, so it scales by adding instances. Guest data stays on the device; accounts use Supabase (Mumbai) with row-level security, and "Delete all my data" really deletes. Court lookups cost ₹0.5-1.5 each, cached for 6 hours and capped per user. Next: a lawyer-reviewed library, verified case law, WhatsApp, and a hand-off to NALSA / Tele-Law with the case file attached.

7. How I'd know it works with real users

The number I'd watch: how many users take the recommended step (send the notice, file the complaint), and a 30-day follow-up asking "did you get your money back?". Alongside it: a lawyer spot-checking 5% of chats and every draft in the pilot (errors per 100), how often the guard steps in, 👎 and report rates per answer, escalations shown vs followed, and where people drop off. I'd pilot with one legal-aid clinic, where people can catch mistakes.

8. AI tools I used, and where they got it wrong

Claude Code for pair programming and research (reading primary sources), Groq gpt-oss-120b / 20b and Whisper and Gemini Flash inside the product. Where they went wrong: (1) The coding assistant's legal knowledge was out of date: it suggested e-Daakhil (replaced by e-Jagriti) and the RBI Ombudsman's old one-year window (now 90 days). Checking primary sources caught both, which is why every library entry carries a URL. (2) In the product, the model told a user "you still have time" after the cheque window had closed, even with the deadline in its prompt; code now adds the alert itself. (3) Asked to answer "like a lawyer", it invented a repainting cost and cited the Model Tenancy Act for Karnataka, which hasn't adopted it. The guard missed both because neither is a section number, so they're banned in the prompt, which is a weaker defence. (4) The router mislabelled a fundamental-rights question, as above. (5) A research agent couldn't read the official Constitution PDF and said so instead of quoting from memory; I then read it myself. Full log: docs/AI_ERRORS_LOG.md.
