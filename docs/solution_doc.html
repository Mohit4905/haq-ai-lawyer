Haq (हक़): AI legal help for everyday problems in India

Problem 2: Build an AI Lawyer · Mohit Piplani · 23113101 · Civil Engineering · App: haq-46bx.onrender.com · Code: github.com/Mohit4905/haq-ai-lawyer · Raw outputs: evals/outputs

1. Why this problem, and how I scoped it

An AI lawyer is where AI is most useful, because people give up on recoverable money for lack of help, and also most dangerous, because models confidently invent law. I treated the second point as the core design problem. When I gave a plain LLM my test questions, it produced "landmark Supreme Court judgments" that a search can't find ("M/s. R. K. M. Industries vs. M/s. R. K. M. Builders, (2005) 2 SCC 462"), cited the IPC (replaced by the BNS on 1 July 2024), and told someone who had missed the 30-day cheque-notice window "Yes, you can still file a case".

Depth over breadth. In depth: security deposit, consumer refund/defect, unpaid salary, bounced cheque, understanding a legal notice. They are common, often winnable without a lawyer, have cheap forums (Consumer Commission fee nil up to ₹5 lakh), and some have strict deadlines (cheque: 30 days) where a wrong answer causes real harm. Pointers only: online/UPI fraud. Not covered (sent to a lawyer / free legal aid): criminal cases against the user, family, property title, tax.

Assumptions (where the brief was silent)
  • User: an adult in India with a phone, possibly not comfortable in English, no lawyer. Haq answers like a lawyer in a consultation (a view, the law, the other side's argument, a plan), but says it is not the user's advocate and when a real one is needed.
  • Law as on Nov 2026, checked against official sources. Important recent changes: Labour Codes in force from 21 Nov 2025 (Payment of Wages Act repealed); e-Jagriti replaced e-Daakhil (Jan 2025); RBI Ombudsman Scheme 2026 (90-day window).
  • State-specific rules (rent Acts, Shops & Establishments, legal-aid income limits) are flagged, not modelled.
  • The user's dates are correct; deadline maths is shown as an estimate with its source.
  • The user sends documents themselves; every draft marks missing facts as [[FILL]].

2. What it does

Chat by text or voice in any Indian language, including Hinglish, replying in the language chosen in the interface. A new problem gets a consultation: my view (how strong, and what it turns on) → law on your side → what they will say, and your answer → what I would do, with timing. A follow-up question gets a direct answer. Helplines appear only when they are the actual next step. As the user talks, Haq builds the case file (facts, timeline, proof held/missing) and computes deadlines in code, each with its source. Drafts in the chat: a deposit/salary/cheque §138 notice, complaint email, Consumer Commission or labour-office complaint, or a reply to a notice appears as a document in the chat, with the missing details asked below it. The user answers in the chat and the blanks fill in (Word/PDF). Live court cases: paste a CNR to see the eCourts record (status, next hearing with calendar export, judge, parties, hearing history, orders). Explain turns an order into a plain-language summary that only restates it. Also: practice hearing in the chat (reply “practice”: AI plays the judge and puts the other side’s case, then scores the user), notice explainer (text/photo/PDF), evidence locker (the user ticks what AI read into the case), My cases (device, or account via email/Google), reminders, and escalation to a lawyer, NALSA 15100, 112 or Tele-MANAS 14416.

Model is a config line. Every provider uses the OpenAI-compatible API (Groq, Gemini, OpenRouter, OpenAI, Anthropic, Ollama, or custom for any endpoint you provide). 4. How it avoids inventing law

  • Verified library: 74 entries (sections, rules, helplines, thresholds), each with an official URL (India Code, e-Gazette, ministries, RBI, NALSA). Only verified entries reach the model.
  • Guard (code) on every reply and draft: blocks sections, Articles, Orders and Acts not in the library (English and Hindi patterns), any case law (X v. Y, AIR/SCC, "the Supreme Court held"), and repealed laws (IPC, CrPC, CPA 1986, Payment of Wages Act…), with the replacement suggested; it also blocks promised outcomes ("you will definitely win", "guaranteed"). An assessment such as "a strong case on these facts" is allowed, as a lawyer would say it. Unsupported time periods are warned on. Then one rewrite, then deletion, and the user is told.
  • Citable law is guaranteed: for a covered problem, if the reply cites no verified provision, code appends the core sections (e.g. NI Act s.138/142 for a cheque).
  • Code safety nets: passed deadlines are always stated; leaked IDs and keys are stripped; emergency numbers are added by code. Each reply shows "2 legal references verified"; a law chip opens its official source.

5. How I know it's good: real runs

22 hand-written cases (5 languages; deposit, consumer, salary, cheque and notice cases; safety, out-of-scope, prompt injection, "will I win?", "give me SC judgments") with automatic checks on category, reply language, extracted facts, must/must-not content, escalation, deadline maths, drafts, and "no unverified law shown". Plus 28 offline unit tests, a library-integrity test, browser tests of the real UI (19-step smoke test; drafting, court, sign-in flows), and 15 security checks. After every run I read every failure by hand and logged it as a bug, a test that was too strict, or model variance (evals/CHANGELOG.md).

In all 4 runs, across 3 models, it told the T11 user "Yes, you can still file a case under Section 138" after the deadline had passed. Note: "flagged" includes real provisions that simply aren't in my library, so it is not a count of fabrications.

Where it fails (honest list)
  • The guard checks citations, not reasoning. A wrong procedural claim slipped through: "bail is applied for in the court that issued the FIR" (police register FIRs). Fluent but wrong advice without any citation is the main residual risk.
  • The regex guard can miss oddly phrased references, and blocks real sections outside the library (over-strict by design).
  • Uncited generalisations and rules stretched beyond their scope, more often in the lawyer style: "the airline can't force a credit shell" (plausible, unverified), the e-commerce 48-hour rule applied to a possibly offline purchase, an invented repainting cost. Fix: claim-level grounding (each legal sentence tagged to a library id) and "applies when" conditions on entries.
  • Small library; no case law yet; RERA and state rules not modelled; free-tier limits (about 40 full answers a day on the main model) push latency to 7–16s. The kanoon.dev judgments search is built but unverified (its pilot API was down).

6. Scale and cost

Measured: ~4.2k input + ~0.9k output tokens and ~2 calls per turn. At the third-party-reported Groq price for gpt-oss-120b ($0.15/$0.60 per M tokens), that's ≈$0.0012 (≈₹0.10) per turn, ≈$6 per 1,000 five-turn conversations. Model cost is negligible. The real costs are legal review of the library (a lawyer signs off each entry; re-verify quarterly) and the human escalation path. Stateless API, so it scales horizontally. Privacy: guests' data stays on the device; accounts use Supabase (Mumbai) with row-level security, verified by 15 checks, and "Delete all my data" really deletes. Court lookups cost ₹0.5–1.5 each (eCourtsIndia credits), cached for 6 hours and capped per user per hour. Next: real case law via Indian Kanoon (only judgments returned by search may be cited), WhatsApp, a hand-off to NALSA/Tele-Law with the case file attached, and state packs.

7. How I'd know it works with real users

North star: % of users who take the recommended action (send the notice, file the complaint), plus a 30-day follow-up: "did you get your money back?". Guardrails: lawyer spot-checks of 5% of conversations and all drafts in the pilot (error rate per 100); guard intervention rate; escalations shown vs followed; drop-off per step; time to first draft. Pilot with one legal-aid clinic, where errors get caught by humans.

8. AI tools I used, and where they got it wrong

Claude Code (pair-programming, research agents to read primary sources), Groq gpt-oss-120b/20b + Whisper and Gemini 3.5 Flash (in the product and as judge). Where they got it wrong: (1) the coding assistant's legal knowledge was out of date (e-Daakhil, since replaced by e-Jagriti; the RBI Ombudsman's old one-year window, now 90 days). Primary sources caught both, so every library entry carries a URL. (2) In-product, the model told a user "you still have time" after the cheque window had closed, with the deadline in its prompt; code now appends the alert. (3) Under a prompt injection it refused to help at all; it now declines only the injected part. (4) Told to answer "like a lawyer", it invented a market price ("repainting costs Rs 8,000–15,000"), an unsourced rule, and cited the Model Tenancy Act for Karnataka, which hasn't adopted it. None of these is a section number, so the guard passed them. They are now banned in the prompt: a weaker defence than code, so it's a residual risk. (5) The eCourts parser I built from the docs showed an internal number as the case number; I caught it on real output. Full log: docs/AI_ERRORS_LOG.md.
