1. Why this problem, and how I scoped it

An AI lawyer is where AI is most useful, because people give up on recoverable money for lack of help, and also most dangerous, because models confidently invent law. I treated the second point as the core design problem. When I gave a plain LLM my test questions, it produced "landmark Supreme Court judgments" that a search can't find ("M/s. R. K. M. Industries vs. M/s. R. K. M. Builders, (2005) 2 SCC 462"), cited the IPC (replaced by the BNS on 1 July 2024), and told someone who had missed the 30-day cheque-notice window "Yes, you can still file a case".

Depth over breadth. In depth: security deposit, consumer refund/defect, unpaid salary, bounced cheque, understanding a legal notice. These are common, usually winnable without a lawyer, have cheap forums (Consumer Commission fee is nil up to ₹5 lakh; labour wage claims; legal notice), and some have strict deadlines (cheque: 30 days), where a wrong answer causes real harm. Pointers only: online/UPI fraud. Not covered (sent to a lawyer / free legal aid): criminal cases against the user, family, property title, tax.

Assumptions (where the brief was silent)
  • User: an adult in India with a phone, possibly not comfortable in English, no lawyer. Haq gives legal information, not advice, and says when a lawyer is needed.
  • Law as on Nov 2026, checked against official sources. Important recent changes: Labour Codes in force from 21 Nov 2025 (Payment of Wages Act repealed); e-Jagriti replaced e-Daakhil (Jan 2025); RBI Ombudsman Scheme 2026 (90-day window).
  • State-specific rules (rent Acts, Shops & Establishments, legal-aid income limits) are flagged, not modelled.
  • The user's dates are correct; deadline maths is shown as an estimate with its source.
  • The user sends documents themselves; every draft marks missing facts as [[FILL]].

2. What it does

Chat by text or voice (Whisper; browser speech as fallback) in any Indian language, including Hinglish and Indian scripts, with read-aloud. As the user talks, Haq: builds the case (live case file: facts, timeline, proof held/missing, completeness meter); explains where they stand and a vetted options ladder, cheapest first; shows deadlines computed by code with the law each comes from; drafts the deposit/salary/cheque §138 notice, complaint email, Consumer Commission complaint, labour-office complaint or a reply to a notice (Word download, "how to send"); runs practice mode (AI plays the landlord/HR/company, then scores the user); explains a notice from pasted text, a photo or a PDF; and escalates to a lawyer, NALSA 15100, 112 or Tele-MANAS 14416.

Model is a config line. Every provider uses the OpenAI-compatible API (Groq, Gemini, OpenRouter, OpenAI, Anthropic, Ollama, or custom for any endpoint you provide). It falls back across models and providers on rate limits, and can be switched live in the UI. Default: gpt-oss-120b (main) and gpt-oss-20b (intake) on Groq's free tier; Gemini as fallback and as the eval judge. The backend is stateless (the client sends the state), so the same engine.turn() serves the UI and the evals.

4. How it avoids inventing law

  • Verified library: 74 entries (sections, rules, helplines, thresholds), each with an official URL (India Code, e-Gazette, ministries, RBI, NALSA). Only verified entries reach the model.
  • Guard (code) on every reply and draft: blocks sections, Articles, Orders and Acts not in the library (English and Hindi patterns), any case law (X v. Y, AIR/SCC, "the Supreme Court held"), and repealed laws (IPC, CrPC, CPA 1986, Payment of Wages Act…), with the replacement suggested. Unsupported time periods are warned on. Then one rewrite, then deletion, and the user is told.
  • Code safety nets: passed deadlines are always stated; leaked IDs and keys are stripped; emergency numbers are added by code.
  • The UI shows "🛡️ 2 legal references verified" on each reply; click a law chip to see its official source.

5. How I know it's good: real runs

22 hand-written cases (5 languages; deposit, consumer, salary, cheque and notice cases; safety, out-of-scope, prompt injection, "will I win?", "give me SC judgments") with automatic checks on category, reply language, extracted facts, must/must-not content, escalation, deadline maths, drafts, and "no unverified law shown". Plus 25 offline unit tests and a library-integrity test. After every run I read every failure by hand and logged it as a bug, a test that was too strict, or model variance (evals/CHANGELOG.md).

Same 22 questions to the same model with a plain "helpful Indian lawyer" prompt (v4): 17/22 answers had a flagged legal reference; 7 cited case law; 7 cited repealed laws (IPC/CrPC, Payment of Wages Act). In all 4 runs, across 3 models, it told the T11 user "Yes, you can still file a case under Section 138" after the deadline had passed. Note: "flagged" includes real provisions that simply aren't in my library, so it is not a count of fabrications.

Where it fails (honest list)
  • The guard checks citations, not reasoning. A wrong procedural claim slipped through: "bail is applied for in the court that issued the FIR" (police register FIRs). Fluent but wrong advice without any citation is the main residual risk.
  • The guard is regex-based: oddly phrased references can slip past it, and real sections outside the library get blocked (over-strict by design).
  • The model ignored a deadline the code had calculated ("you still have time"); fixed by extracting facts first and adding a code-written alert. Language drift (Hinglish ↔ English) is fixed by a code check plus a rewrite.
  • Uncited generalisations and rules stretched beyond their scope (manual review of v4): "the airline can't force a credit shell" and "the principal employer is liable if the contractor vanishes" are plausible but unverified; the e-commerce 48-hour rule was applied to a laptop that may have been bought offline. Fix: claim-level grounding (every legal sentence tagged to a library id, untagged ones flagged) and "applies when" conditions on library entries.
  • Small library; no case law at all; RERA and state rules not modelled; free-tier rate limits push median latency to 7–16s (4s at best).

6. Scale and cost

Measured: ~4.2k input + ~0.9k output tokens and ~2 calls per turn. At the third-party-reported Groq price for gpt-oss-120b ($0.15/$0.60 per M tokens), that's ≈$0.0012 (≈₹0.10) per turn, ≈$6 per 1,000 five-turn conversations. Model cost is negligible. The real costs are legal review of the library (a lawyer signs off each entry; re-verify quarterly; this run alone found 4 recent changes) and the human escalation path. Stateless API, so it scales horizontally. Next: WhatsApp channel, deadline reminders, a hand-off to NALSA/Tele-Law with the case file attached, state packs, and verified landmark judgments.

7. How I'd know it works with real users

North star: % of users who take the recommended action (send the notice, file the complaint), plus a 30-day follow-up: "did you get your money back?". Guardrails: lawyer spot-checks of 5% of conversations and all drafts in the pilot (error rate per 100); guard intervention rate; escalations shown vs followed; drop-off per step; time to first draft. Pilot with one legal-aid clinic, where errors get caught by humans.

8. AI tools I used, and where they got it wrong

Claude Code (pair-programming, research agents to read primary sources), Groq gpt-oss-120b/20b + Whisper and Gemini 3.5 Flash (in the product and as judge). Where they got it wrong: (1) the coding assistant's legal knowledge was out of date: it pointed users to e-Daakhil (replaced by e-Jagriti) and assumed the RBI Ombudsman's old one-year window (now 90 days). Primary-source checks caught both, which is why every library entry carries a URL. (2) Its default model names (llama-3.3-70b, gemini-2.5-flash) no longer exist on free tiers, so model names are now read from the API. (3) In-product: even with the calculated deadline in its prompt, the model told a user "you still have time" after the cheque window had closed. Fix: facts are extracted first, deadlines computed in code, and code appends the alert if the reply omits it. (4) Under a prompt injection it refused to help at all; it now declines only the injected part. (5) The LLM judge marked correct recent-law answers as wrong, so I don't trust a judge on fresh law.
