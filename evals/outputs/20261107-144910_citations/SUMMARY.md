### 1. Explain the Right to Equality.
_model: gemini/gemini-3.1-flash-lite_

**In short:** The Right to Equality is a fundamental right guaranteed by the Constitution of India, ensuring that every person is treated equally before the law and prohibiting discrimination by the State.

**The law that applies:**
* **Article 14, Constitution of India:** Guarantees equality before the law and equal protection of the laws to every person within India. This is the bedrock of the right.
* **Article 15, Constitution of India:** Prohibits the State from discriminating against any citizen on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth. It also ensures access to public places like shops and hotels.
* **Article 16, Constitution of India:** Ensures equality of opportunity in matters of public employment. The State cannot discriminate in government jobs based on the grounds mentioned above.
* **Article 17, Constitution of India:** Abolishes 'untouchability' and forbids its practice in any form.
* **Article 18, Constitution of India:** Abolishes titles (except military or academic distinctions) to ensure social equality.

**What it means for you:** In practice, this means the government cannot create laws or policies that arbitrarily favour one group over another. For example, if a government scheme is open to the public, it cannot exclude you simply because of your religion or gender.

**If your right is violated:** Because these are Fundamental Rights, you can approach the High Court under **Article 226** or the Supreme Court under **Article 32** of the Constitution to seek a remedy (a writ) if the State violates these rights.

**Keep in mind:** These rights are primarily enforceable against 'the State' (government bodies and authorities), not private individuals. Also, the State is permitted to make special provisions for women, children, and socially or educationally backward classes to ensure substantive equality.

The Right to Freedom of Speech and Expression is a Fundamental Right guaranteed to all citizens of India under the Constitution. It allows you to express your opinions, beliefs, and ideas freely, including through speech, writing, printing, or any other medium.

**Keep in mind:**

### 4. Can the police arrest me without a warrant?
_model: gemini/gemini-3.1-flash-lite_

**In short:** Yes, the police can arrest you without a warrant, but only in specific situations involving 'cognizable' offences—serious crimes where the law allows them to act immediately to prevent further harm or secure evidence. This power is strictly regulated to protect your liberty.

**The law that applies:**
* **Section 35, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023:** This section defines when police can arrest without a warrant. It applies to 'cognizable' offences (serious crimes). The officer must have a reasonable suspicion or a credible complaint that you committed the offence, and they must believe the arrest is necessary—for example, to stop you from committing another crime, to prevent you from tampering with evidence, or to ensure you are available for trial.
* **Article 22, Constitution of India:** This is your fundamental protection. If you are arrested, you must be informed of the grounds for your arrest as soon as possible. You have the right to consult and be defended by a lawyer of your choice, and you must be produced before the nearest Magistrate within 24 hours.
* **Section 47, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023:** If the offence is bailable, the police are legally required to inform you that you are entitled to be released on bail and that you can arrange for sureties.

**What it means for you:** In practice, this means police cannot just pick you up for minor disputes or non-cognizable offences without a Magistrate's order. If they do arrest you, they must follow the procedure: tell you why, inform your family or a nominated person, and take you to a Magistrate within 24 hours.

**If your right is violated:** If you are arrested illegally or without being told the grounds, you or your family can approach the High Court under **Article 226, Constitution of India** for a writ of *habeas corpus* to challenge the detention. You should immediately ask for a lawyer.

**Keep in mind:** The police cannot hold you for more than 24 hours without a Magistrate's order. Always ask for the grounds of arrest and contact a lawyer or family member immediately.

**The law that applies:**

**What it means for you:** This law applies to any theft committed on or after 1 July 2024. For any theft that occurred before this date, the old Indian Penal Code (IPC) would apply instead.

### 9. Can my employer terminate me without notice?

In short: Generally, no. If you are a 'worker' under the law, your employer cannot simply terminate you without notice or pay in lieu of notice. This is a protection provided under the Industrial Relations Code, 2020, which came into force on 21 November 2025.

**The law that applies:**
* **Section 70, Industrial Relations Code, 2020**: This section states that a worker with at least one year of continuous service cannot be retrenched (terminated) unless they are given one month's written notice with reasons, or one month's wages in place of that notice. They must also be paid compensation of 15 days' average pay for every completed year of service.
* **Section 2(zr), Industrial Relations Code, 2020**: This defines who is a 'worker'. It includes people employed for manual, unskilled, skilled, technical, operational, clerical or supervisory work. It excludes those in managerial or administrative roles, or supervisors earning above ₹18,000 per month (or the government-notified limit).

**What it means for you:**
If you fall under the definition of a 'worker', your employer must follow the procedure of giving notice or paying wages in lieu of notice. If they terminate you immediately without this, they are likely violating the law. Note that this does not apply to fixed-term employment contracts that have reached their natural end date.

**If your right is violated / What you can do:**
If you have been terminated without notice or compensation, you can approach the Labour Commissioner's office in your area to file a complaint. You may also seek legal advice to send a formal demand notice to your employer.

**Keep in mind:**
This protection applies to 'workers' as defined by the Code. If you are in a senior managerial or administrative role, your rights are primarily governed by the terms of your employment contract rather than these specific provisions of the Industrial Relations Code. Always check your appointment letter for the specific notice period agreed upon.

### 10. What does Article 21 of the Indian Constitution say?
_model: gemini/gemini-3.1-flash-lite_

**In short:** Article 21 is a fundamental right that guarantees that no person shall be deprived of their life or personal liberty except according to the procedure established by law. It is considered the heart of the Indian Constitution, as it protects the most basic human rights.

**The law that applies:**
* **Article 21, Constitution of India**: This article states that "No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law." It applies to every person, not just citizens.

**What it means for you:** In practice, this means the government cannot take away your life or freedom arbitrarily. Over time, the courts have interpreted this article very broadly to include many other rights essential for a dignified life, such as the right to privacy, the right to a clean environment, the right to education, and the right to a speedy trial.

**If your right is violated / What you can do:** If your fundamental rights under Article 21 are violated by the State or a public authority, you can approach the High Court under **Article 226** or the Supreme Court under **Article 32** of the Constitution to seek protection through a writ petition. These courts have the power to issue orders to stop such violations.

**Keep in mind:** While this right is extremely powerful, it is not absolute. The government can restrict your liberty, but only through a "procedure established by law"—meaning there must be a valid, fair, and reasonable law in place that justifies the action. If you believe your rights are being infringed, it is best to consult a legal professional to determine if the specific action taken against you follows the required legal process.
