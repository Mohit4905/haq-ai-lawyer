### 1. Explain the Right to Equality.
_model: gemini/gemini-3.1-flash-lite_

**In short:** The Right to Equality is a fundamental right guaranteed by the Constitution of India, ensuring that every person is treated equally before the law and protected from discrimination by the State.

**The law that applies:**
* **Article 14, Constitution of India:** This guarantees equality before the law and equal protection of the laws to any person within India. It is the cornerstone of the right to equality.
* **Article 15, Constitution of India:** This prohibits the State from discriminating against any citizen on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth. It also ensures equal access to public places like shops, hotels, and wells.
* **Article 16, Constitution of India:** This ensures equality of opportunity in matters of public employment. The State cannot discriminate against citizens for government jobs based on the grounds mentioned in Article 15.
* **Article 17, Constitution of India:** This abolishes 'untouchability' and forbids its practice in any form, making it a punishable offence.
* **Article 18, Constitution of India:** This prohibits the State from conferring titles (except military or academic distinctions) to prevent the creation of artificial hierarchies.

**What it means for you:** In practice, this means the government cannot make laws or policies that arbitrarily favour one group over another or treat people differently without a valid, reasonable justification. For example, if a government scheme provides benefits, it must be accessible to all eligible citizens without bias based on their caste or religion.

**If your right is violated:** If the State (government authorities, public bodies) violates these rights, you can approach the High Court under **Article 226** or the Supreme Court under **Article 32** of the Constitution to seek enforcement of your fundamental rights.

### 4. Can the police arrest me without a warrant?
_model: gemini/gemini-3.1-flash-lite_

Yes, the police can arrest you without a warrant, but only in specific, defined situations. They cannot simply pick you up at their whim.

**In short:** Under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023, the police have the power to arrest without a warrant for 'cognizable offences'—serious crimes where they can investigate without a Magistrate's order. However, this power is strictly regulated to prevent abuse.

**The law that applies:**
*   **Section 35, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023:** This section lists the situations where police can arrest without a warrant. It includes cases where a cognizable offence is committed in their presence, or where there is a credible complaint or reasonable suspicion of a cognizable offence punishable with up to 7 years, provided the officer believes the arrest is necessary to prevent further crime or tampering with evidence.
*   **Article 22, Constitution of India:** This is your fundamental protection. If you are arrested, you must be informed of the grounds for your arrest immediately. You have the right to consult a lawyer of your choice and must be produced before a Magistrate within 24 hours.
*   **Section 47 & 48, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023:** These sections mandate that the police must inform you of the grounds of arrest and your right to bail (if the offence is bailable). They are also legally required to inform a relative or friend of your choice about your arrest and location.

**What it means for you:** In practice, if the police approach you, they must be able to justify why the arrest is necessary under the law. They cannot hold you for more than 24 hours without presenting you before a Magistrate.

**If your right is violated:** If you are detained illegally or without being told the grounds, you or your family can approach the High Court under **Article 226** of the Constitution for a writ of *habeas corpus* to demand your production in court. 

**Keep in mind:** This is a general overview. If you are facing an immediate threat of arrest, you should contact a lawyer or legal aid immediately, as the specific facts of the case determine the legality of the police action.

### 5. What is the punishment for theft in India?

**In short:** Theft is a criminal offence under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, which has replaced the Indian Penal Code since 1 July 2024. The punishment depends on the nature of the theft and whether it is a repeat offence.

**The law that applies:**
*   **Section 303, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023:** This section defines theft as dishonestly moving someone's movable property out of their possession without consent, with the intention of taking it. It prescribes imprisonment of up to 3 years, or a fine, or both. For a second or subsequent conviction, the punishment increases to rigorous imprisonment of 1 to 5 years and a fine.

**What it means for you:**
In practice, if someone steals your property, the law treats it as a serious offence. The police are required to register an FIR (First Information Report) if the offence is cognizable. If the stolen property is of low value (less than ₹5,000), the law provides for a summary trial process, which is generally faster.

**If your right is violated / What you can do:**
If you are a victim of theft, you should report the incident immediately to the nearest police station. Under **Section 173 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023**, you can file a 'Zero FIR' at any police station, regardless of where the theft actually occurred, if you are unable to reach the specific station with jurisdiction. You can also report crimes through the emergency number 112.

**Keep in mind:**
This information is based on the new criminal laws (BNS and BNSS) that came into effect on 1 July 2024. Offences committed before this date are still governed by the old Indian Penal Code (IPC). Always ensure you have any available evidence, such as CCTV footage or witness details, when reporting the crime.

**Keep in mind:**

### 9. Can my employer terminate me without notice?
_model: gemini/gemini-3.1-flash-lite_

In short, it depends on your employment contract and your role. Generally, an employer cannot simply terminate a 'worker' without following the procedure laid down in the Industrial Relations Code, 2020, which includes providing notice or pay in lieu of notice. 

**The law that applies:**
* **Section 70, Industrial Relations Code, 2020:** This section mandates that a worker with at least one year of continuous service cannot be retrenched without one month's written notice (or wages in lieu of notice) and compensation of 15 days' average pay for every completed year of service. 
* **Section 2(zr), Industrial Relations Code, 2020:** This defines who is a 'worker'. It covers most manual, skilled, technical, or clerical roles but excludes those in managerial or administrative capacities, or supervisors earning above a certain threshold (currently ₹18,000 per month).
* **Section 2(zh), Industrial Relations Code, 2020:** This defines 'retrenchment' as the termination of service by an employer for any reason other than disciplinary punishment. It excludes fixed-term contracts that have naturally expired.

**What it means for you:**
If you fall under the definition of a 'worker' and your termination is not due to disciplinary action or the end of a fixed-term contract, your employer is legally required to provide notice or pay in lieu of it. If you are in a managerial role, your rights are governed primarily by the terms of your employment contract rather than these specific statutory protections.

**If your right is violated:**
If you believe you were wrongfully terminated, you can approach the Labour Commissioner's office in your area or seek legal advice to challenge the termination. 

**Keep in mind:**
Since 21 November 2025, the four Labour Codes, including the Industrial Relations Code, 2020, are in force. Always check your appointment letter first, as it often contains specific notice period clauses that may be more generous than the statutory minimums.

### 10. What does Article 21 of the Indian Constitution say?
_model: gemini/gemini-3.1-flash-lite_

**In short:** Article 21 is the "Right to Life and Personal Liberty," a fundamental right guaranteed by the Constitution of India. It ensures that no one can be deprived of their life or personal freedom unless the government follows a fair and just legal procedure.

**The law that applies:**
* **Article 21, Constitution of India:** This article states that no person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. It is the bedrock of individual rights in India, protecting you against arbitrary state action.

**What it means for you:** In practice, this is much broader than just "not being killed." Courts have interpreted this to include the right to live with human dignity, which covers rights like the right to privacy, a clean environment, education, and a speedy trial. If the state tries to take away your liberty—like arresting you or restricting your movement—they must have a valid law and follow a fair process.

**If your right is violated / What you can do:** If the state violates this right, you can approach the High Court under Article 226 or the Supreme Court under Article 32 to seek protection.

**Keep in mind:** This right applies to everyone in India, not just citizens. However, it is not absolute; the state can restrict it, but only through a law that is reasonable, fair, and not arbitrary.
