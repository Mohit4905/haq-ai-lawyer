### 1. What are my fundamental rights?
_legal → legal_

Fundamental rights are the core protections provided by the Constitution of India to every person. Think of these as the legal framework that defines your relationship with the State, which includes the government and public authorities.

Here are the key rights provided:

* **Equality and Non-Discrimination:** You have the right to equality before the law. The State cannot discriminate against you based on religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth. Untouchability is abolished and forbidden in any form.
* **Freedoms:** You have the right to freedom of speech and expression, to assemble peacefully, to form associations, to move freely throughout India, and to practise any profession or trade.
* **Life and Liberty:** No person can be deprived of their life or personal liberty except according to the procedure established by law. If arrested, you have the right to be informed of the grounds, to consult a lawyer, and to be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours.
* **Protection against Exploitation:** Trafficking in human beings and forced labour (begar) are prohibited.
* **Religion and Education:** You have the freedom to profess, practise, and propagate your religion. The State is also required to provide free and compulsory education to children aged six to fourteen.
* **Enforcement:** The right to approach the Supreme Court to enforce these fundamental rights is provided under Article 32 of the Constitution.

These rights are primarily enforceable against the State. If you believe your rights have been violated, you can seek legal advice to understand your specific remedy. If you are unsure about how these apply to your situation, you can call the NALSA legal aid helpline at 15100 for guidance.

### 2. What is the difference between BNS and IPC?
_legal → legal_

Think of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) as the modern successor to the Indian Penal Code (IPC). The IPC was the backbone of our criminal law for over 160 years, but it was a colonial-era document. The BNS, which came into effect on 1 July 2024, has replaced it to better reflect contemporary Indian society and legal needs.

*   **The Transition:** The BNS is not just a renaming; it is a complete overhaul. It repeals the IPC entirely. However, it is important to remember that the BNS is not retrospective. This means that any offence committed before 1 July 2024 will still be investigated and tried under the old IPC. 
*   **Key Changes:** The BNS has streamlined many provisions, introduced new categories of offences (such as those related to organized crime and terrorism), and updated punishments to be more in line with current standards. It also focuses on faster justice delivery.
*   **Practical Impact:** If you are dealing with a legal issue today, you must check the date of the incident. If the incident happened on or after 1 July 2024, the BNS applies. If it happened before that date, the IPC remains the governing law for that specific case.

I do not quote specific case judgments as none are verified in my library yet, but the principle of 'non-retroactivity' is a fundamental rule of criminal law—you cannot be punished under a new law for an act that was committed when that law did not exist.

### 3. How can I file an FIR?
_legal → legal_

Filing an FIR (First Information Report) is the first step to report a cognizable offence—serious crimes like theft, assault, or fraud—to the police. Under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), which replaced the old CrPC from 1 July 2024, the process is straightforward:

* **Where to go:** You can go to any police station to report a cognizable offence, even if the incident happened outside their jurisdiction. This is called a 'Zero FIR', which the police must register and then transfer to the correct station.
* **How to report:** You can give the information orally or in writing. If you give it orally, the officer must write it down. You have the right to have it read back to you to ensure it is accurate before you sign it.
* **e-FIR:** For certain offences, you may also be able to file an e-FIR online through your state police portal. If you do this, you must sign it electronically or physically within three days for it to be valid.
* **Your rights:** Once the FIR is registered, the police must provide you with a free copy of it immediately. If a police station refuses to register your FIR, you can approach the Superintendent of Police (SP) or the Deputy Commissioner of Police (DCP) in writing to request action.

If you are in an emergency, you can always dial 112 for immediate assistance.

### 5. What should I do if someone commits online fraud?
_legal → legal_

If you have been a victim of online fraud, time is your biggest enemy. You must act immediately to increase the chances of freezing the stolen money.

1. **Call 1930 immediately:** This is the national cybercrime helpline. Reporting here quickly is the most effective way to alert banks and potentially freeze the transaction before the money moves further.
2. **Report online:** Visit the official National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal at [cybercrime.gov.in](https://www.cybercrime.gov.in/) and file a formal complaint. Keep the acknowledgement number safe.
3. **Inform your bank:** Contact your bank's customer care or visit your branch immediately to report the unauthorized transaction. Ask them to block your account or card if necessary.
4. **File a police complaint:** If the bank or the portal requires it, you can visit your nearest police station to file an FIR. Remember, under the new law (Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023), you can file a 'Zero FIR' at any police station, regardless of where the crime actually occurred.

Do not wait for the fraudster to reply or for the 'customer care' of the fake site to resolve it. If you have lost a significant amount, I strongly recommend you also consult a local lawyer to ensure all legal avenues are covered.
